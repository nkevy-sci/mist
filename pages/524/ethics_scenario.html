Ethics Scenario

IST524

On Shortcuts at Work

Accessibility

When eLearning is designed, the accessibility of instructional content must be carefully considered so that it benefits learners regardless of their ability. Indeed, an instructional designer is obligated "to protect [any] individual learner's rights of access," ensuring to them the opportunity to learn (Section 1, AECT Ethics Committee, 2018). As well, research has shown that unequal access harms learning environments. For if a learner cannot access a "learning task" their motivation to learn may falter (Sewell et al., 2022, p. 370). Clearly, impediments to access violate the professional commitment an instructional designer has for a learner (Section 1, AECT, 2018). And, in many institutional contexts whether they are public or private, undue impediments are illegal (U.S. Access Board, n.d.). Equal access to learning opportunities is both a human right and enforced by law.

Plus, the consideration of accessibility of instructional design improves eLearning for all learners, not just for those whom need it. Sometimes called "the curb cut effect," the general benefit of accessibility is well documented and practical. Universal Design for Learning (UDL), also known as Universal Instructional Design, organizes the design process so that every learner feels included when they engage with the resulting learning environment (Rothwell et al., 2016, p. 88). For example, often, learners engage with text-based materials. Designing for accessibility would emphasize readability, good color contrast, and access to variable fonts (and font sizes). Increased readability, for all learners, makes learning more efficient than it otherwise would be. Also, UDL guidelines encourage designers/educators to create "multiple means of engagement," for each means is an opportunity for learning (Sewell et al., 2022, p. 365). Screen readers similarly effect text-based materials by allowing learners to switch modalities: They can read, or they can engage with text by listening to it.

A Vignette

To demonstrate an application of accessibility to eLearning, consider the following vignette. Jack and Jill are working on an eLearning module, but their product might not be done before the deadline. Jack, to save time, suggests that a review of the accessibility of their online learning course should be skipped. Because Jack is the acting-project manager he can ensure their product passes any later evaluations (e.g. tests for quality assurance). What should Jill do? Clearly, Jill is required to ensure equal access to learning. So what ethical decisions can she make? First, Jill should reassure Jack that their online learning module will be completed on time because Jack and Jill can reduce its scope. Gently, and second, Jill should remind Jack both of their legal obligations and of the AECT code of ethics (a similar code will do).

Jill is an experienced designer. She knows how to design for accessibility to increase the efficiency, effectiveness and engagement of their online learning module (Merrill, 2025, p. 61). Jack and Jill utilize a template inspired by M. David Merrill's Tell-Show-Do sequence, reducing any remaining content to these three steps. Then, "could we begin our accessibility review immediately?" she asks Jack. Inspired by UDL, Jill provides multiple means of engaging with their existing content. Afterwards, Jack and Jill identify and remove "barriers to learning," submitting a reduced yet effective online learning product (Sewell et al., 2022, p. 369).

Conclusion

This vignette shows that a commitment to accessibility will improve online learning environments for all learners. Conversely, a shortcut would jeopardize learning and the credibility of Jack and Jill's designs. If they were to forgo an accessibility review they certainly would be penalized according to either the code of ethics that has standing in this case or the law that is enforced be it state or federal. Obviously, such penalties would cost Jack and Jill more than a missed deadline.
