MIST for Canvas Integration & Analytics

The Master of Instructional Science and Technology (MIST) program at California State University Monterey Bay partnered with the Collaborative Language Program (CLP) of the University of Wisconsin Green Bay (UWGB) to support the redesign of German 101, an introductory German language course. Over eight weeks, a small team of graduate students worked with the instructor of German 101 to redesign the course.

Team

The client, subject matter expert, and instructor was Dr. David Coury, a professor of German language studies with extensive experience teaching German. He has worked at the CLP for a number of years, long enough to see it grow and change.

The instructional design team consisted of Noah Kevy, Ahmed Raoof, and Mauricio Vega. Team members worked collaboratively; however, each team member was assigned a job on which to focus.

  • David, SME
  • Noah, Project Manager
  • Ahmed, SME, Lead Developer
  • Mauricio, Graphic Designer, Supporting Developer

Teamwork was facilitated by a weekly meeting and regular communication. The meetings were hosted on Zoom and held at 10 AM PST, 7 PM CET. One of the team members was living in Europe for the duration of the project. Team members communicated over Google Chat. Generally, the workload was divided and shared. As problems arose, collaborative problem solving took place over chat.

Project Overview

The Collaborative Language Program serves students from multiple institutions across the University of Wisconsin System. It does so by delivering language curriculum online, often employing a blended learning approach. The CLP’s mission is to provide equitable access to language education. German 101 contributes to the mission of the CLP by delivering a high-quality German language arts course to undergraduate students who are geographically dispersed across the state of Wisconsin.

The primary goal of the project was to redesign German 101 by integrating an open educational resource (OER), Willkommen: Deutsch für alle, into Canvas, a learning management system (LMS). The course was to present instructional materials, assignments, discussions, and assessments in a single location. By embedding the OER directly into Canvas, the MIST team sought to create a seamless learning environment, which would minimize switching tabs and improve the overall student experience.

The MIST team did not develop a curriculum; instead, the team adopted the pedagogical framework established by Claudia Kost and Crystal Sawatzk, the authors of the OER. Willkommen follows a communicative, task-based approach to language learning that emphasizes authentic communication and meaningful interaction. Maintaining this instructional philosophy was important because it aligned with current research in second language acquisition. In addition, the team developed supporting resources that enhanced usability, accessibility, and learner engagement.

The learners are undergraduate students with little to no prior experience in the German language. They come from a variety of institutions, including both campuses within the UW System and non-affiliated campuses. Students choose to take German 101 for a variety of reasons. Some are fulfilling a university language requirement, while others are interested in learning the language for personal enrichment.

Learning & Organizational Goals

The instructional goal of German 101 is to provide beginning language learners with a strong foundation in German by developing the four essential language skills of listening, speaking, reading, and writing. Instruction will gradually introduce vocabulary, grammar, pronunciation, and cultural knowledge through meaningful communicative experiences.

Both the instructional goals and the objectives outlined in Willkommen contributed to the following list of learning outcomes.

By the end of German 101, learners will be able to:

  • Recognize the German alphabet.
  • Pronounce the German letters, letter combinations, and words.
  • Use numbers confidently while speaking or writing German.
  • Greet others and introduce themselves using common everyday expressions in German.
  • Use basic present-tense verbs to communicate simple ideas and information.
  • Talk about family members and describe personal relationships.
  • Describe their likes, dislikes, hobbies, and leisure activities in German.
  • Read and understand short texts written for beginning German learners.
  • Write simple sentences and brief paragraphs using basic vocabulary and grammar.
  • Participate in basic conversations and classroom activities using German.
  • Demonstrate an understanding of cultural traditions, perspectives and practices in German-speaking countries.
  • Actively participate in discussions, demonstrating their language skills.

There are three course-level goals. The first goal was to create a fully online learning environment that facilitated both synchronous and asynchronous learning. A second goal was to reduce the cost of instructional materials by utilizing an OER. Third, to ensure high-quality educational experiences, learning activities were aligned with assignments. In response to the widespread use of artificial intelligence software, German 101 continued its shift toward oral assessments.

Design Evaluation

The initial design of German 101, the prototype, was evaluated using a modified talk-aloud protocol. Two participants volunteered their time. One was a former student of German 101 who had just graduated, and the other was a graduate student in MIST who spoke some German. The data were qualitative, the results of observations. Two rounds of testing were conducted. One participant tested the initial prototype, the team revised it, and then both participants tested a second iteration.

Participants engaged with the prototype while narrating their experience. Also, they participated in an unstructured interview. The interview questions were guided by an empathy mapping approach; the instrument is included in Appendix A. However, one participant took running notes and video recorded their screen instead of talking aloud. The team watched their video before interviewing them. Thus, empathy mapping was used to modify the standard talk-aloud approach. Participants engaged with Einheit 2.1 and 2.2.

Results

Notes were taken during both observations and interviews in order to discover what worked and what didn’t. Clear problems or barriers to usability were prioritized. As well, after reading and organizing interview notes, a short list of themes was produced. Themes on which both participants commented are included in Table 1.

The primary obstacle to usability had to do with assignment submission. During the first round of testing one participant felt that requiring screenshots of each interactive activity was a burden; there were simply “too many screenshots.” Yet, because they had recorded their screen, the team realized that video recordings could be used to collect evidence. This led to the development of a submission procedure. Students would create a video recording, showing that they had completed the activities. When this procedure was tested, both participants felt it was a good solution.

The identification of themes provided insight into the student experience. It is worth noting that both participants found working with the OER enjoyable. The former student wished that their course had been as much fun. As well, both participants spoke German aloud during the interviews. When writing, participants had to copy-paste non-English characters from the textbook, and one said that a German keyboard would be nice. Both participants felt that the iframe drew their focus. As a result, the start and reply buttons were ignored until the participants realized that they had to submit their work somehow.

The interpretation of these themes led to the further development of each unit. Instructions were written and placed above each embedded page of the OER. In the second round of testing, the participants requested that instructions be placed at the bottom of the page also (that is, both above and below the iframe). In addition to pointing out how to begin the assignment or discussion, instructions clarify what the student is expected to write if activities in a given unit require a written response. This request was not implemented, yet it has been communicated to the client.

Course-Level Evaluation

When the course is implemented, students will be asked to comment on the course at the end of Unit 3. That is, approximately halfway through the course students will be asked to complete a short survey. There is some evidence that course evaluations conducted during a course have a better response rate than those at the end of the course (Kirkpatrick & Kirkpatrick, 2006, p. 100).

Evaluations of learning will take place throughout the course, primarily taking the form of formative evaluation. Short, low-point-value oral exams were placed at the end of each unit. As well, the instructor plans to have a midterm and a final. Both will rely heavily on virtual face-to-face interactions between the student and the instructor. As such, the oral exams serve as authentic preparation for cumulative tests.

Discussion of behavioral change and return on investment were unfortunately limited. While these are important concerns, functionality was prioritized. The team did briefly discuss that some students would be moving from German 101 to intermediate German courses. The number of students who continue with the program may provide a means to measure both long-term behavioral change and return on investment.

Design Decisions

The first guiding decision was the selection of a design model. The Successive Approximation Model (SAM) was selected because the team had a small amount of time relative to the amount of work required to finish the development of a college-level German language arts course. In the first days of the project, the team members each took a unit and attempted to develop a solution. This approach allowed the team to discover problems and compare potential solutions during the second week of the project.

As the project continued, weekly meetings allowed the client to review prototypes, provide feedback, and request modifications before additional development occurred. This collaborative approach ensured that instructional materials remained aligned with the pedagogical goals of German 101 while allowing the design team to respond quickly to technical limitations, scheduling constraints, and evolving project requirements.

The final design consisted of five Canvas modules corresponding to the five Einheiten. Each module contained embedded textbook activities, instructional pages, discussions, assignments, and supporting resources, which guided students through the learning process. To maintain consistency throughout the course, every module follows the same organizational pattern. Students begin by reviewing the learning objectives before engaging with the OER.

As the redesign continued, the importance of accessibility and student support came to the fore. A few student supports were developed. For example, testing revealed that assignment submission was new and unfamiliar. The team developed instructions, explaining how students could video record their computer screens and submit videos as evidence that they completed their interactive activities. As another example, the team wrote instructions that showed which Canvas notification settings should be turned on.

Challenges

The biggest challenge for the team was time. With just eight weeks to redesign an entire introductory language course, the team had to balance careful instructional design with a fast production schedule. This time pressure led the team to choose the SAM design model, which made it possible to quickly prototype, test, and revise instead of following a slower, step-by-step process. Working as a distributed team made things even harder, since one member was in Europe for the whole project. Weekly meetings had to work around a nine-hour time difference, so the team relied on Google Chat and emails to keep work moving between meetings.

Another set of challenges came up during usability testing and the integration process. The original way students submitted assignments, which involved taking screenshots of each interactive activity, turned out to be too much work for them. Partway through the project, the team switched to a video-based submission method. Embedded iframes also distracted students from important navigation buttons, so the team added extra instructions above each activity.

More generally, bringing an existing OER into Canvas without being the original curriculum designers, while keeping its communicative, task-based approach meant the team had to keep balancing technical limits, client needs, and best teaching practices throughout the eight weeks, instead of planning for LMS integration at the start.

Advice for Future Students

Communicate early and often. At a minimum, set up a weekly meeting with the client. While some meetings may need to be an hour long, many meetings can be as short as 20 minutes.

If project management software will be used, start sooner rather than later. Perhaps it is best to start during the first week, as soon as a timeline is drafted. Some students may be able to draft their timeline directly inside of a project management platform. This approach will reduce workload, improve adoption, and increase the amount of time left to explore the selected project management software.

Give the client what they want. Advanced Instructional Design, this course, provides a robust framework for defining desired outcomes. This approach is invaluable. However, as a project continues, many ideas about how to achieve success will emerge. The client will approve of some ideas and be less fond of others. While it is important to recommend best practices, a customer service mindset takes precedence. Good ideas that don’t make the cut can be briefly written about. Providing the client with recommendations for future development is like planting a seed. At a later time, they may see its value.

Summary

The MIST team worked for Dr. David Coury and the UW Collaborative Learning Program to develop an online, introductory course to the German language. Decisions made over the course of the eight-week development cycle were guided by an overarching mission: to provide equitable access to language education. The final design successfully integrated an open educational resource, an online textbook, into Canvas, enhancing the student’s learning experience by improving their online learning environment.

Of course, German 101 will continue to develop. Oral exams will be scripted and sourcing additional materials may be required to serve the dynamic, in-the-moment needs of students. Also during implementation, course-level formative evaluation will be conducted so that German 101 can continually improve. Future students of Advanced Instructional Design should be ready to engage in frequent communication with their client, for it makes collaboration not only possible but worthwhile. The MIST team enjoyed the opportunity to support the CLP in its pursuit of accessible foreign language education.

Appendix A

Interview Protocol for Testing German 101

This document is used to facilitate the formative evaluation of instructional materials designed for German 101, an introductory course on German language arts.

The interview uses an empathy mapping approach alongside a modified talk-aloud protocol. Simply put, the participants’ actions are video recorded. Then the interviewer and the participant watch the sections of the recording and talk about their experience. If audio is not recorded (as is the case here) it is recommended that the participant type their initial reactions while they engage with the prototype.

Supplies

  • Zoom
  • Protocol for the interviewer(s)

Goals

The primary goal of this interview is to discover barriers to learning. If there are elements of the current design that impede its accessibility, usability, reliability, or face validity, the team needs to know so that these elements can be redesigned. The secondary goal is to think from the perspective of the participant; their specific interactions with the materials may lead to new and innovative designs.

Objectives

  • The participant will explain their previously recorded actions.
  • The interviewer will prompt for further explanation by completing an empathy map.

Interview Introduction

I/We will be talking to you today about the instructional design of German 101. I/We have been working to improve the course for future students. I’m glad you're willing to share your experience with me. Thank you. What I/we really need is your honest opinion. After we talk through the lesson, I’ll ask if there is anything more you’d like to share.

Questions

As we watch you work through Einheiten [UNIT NUMBER], please think back and explain what you were doing and why.

More questions. Could you say more about that? What parts of the design were clear? What was confusing and why? Did you feel any delight or frustration or any emotion at all?
